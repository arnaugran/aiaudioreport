# Editorial guide

Read this before writing anything for AI Audio Report. It is the scope, the
voice and the evidence rule in one place. The build enforces the schema and the
photograph rule; this file covers what the build can't check.

## Scope (widened 17 November 2026)

The site started as dated prices and licence terms. That stays, but it is one
pillar now, not the whole site. A producer on a budget asks more than "what does
it cost", and the same evidence habit answers the harder questions better than
the sites currently ranking for them.

1. **Rights and licensing.** What published terms actually permit: AI output,
   samples, voice cloning, distribution rules.
2. **Pricing and cost maths.** Plans, credits, editions, rent versus buy.
3. **Technique and workflow.** How to do a specific job well, built on primary
   documentation: platform delivery specs (Spotify, Apple, YouTube, distributors),
   loudness and true peak, sample rate and bit depth for a given destination,
   stem separation workflows, cleaning up a vocal rip, using a DAW's own AI
   features, file formats, metadata. Example: `spotify-loudness-normalization-master`.
4. **How things work.** Explainers an intermediate producer can act on: what a
   loudness meter measures, why a separated stem has artefacts, what a codec does
   to a master, what a licence clause means in practice.
5. **Fit and decision.** Which tool or edition for a named situation.

Topic choice: prefer a specific question someone types into Google over a broad
review. Good: "what Spotify does to a quiet master", "which Cubase edition has
stem separation", "how to export stems for a mixing engineer in Ableton".
Bad: "best mastering plugins 2026", "ultimate guide to mixing".

Technique pieces carry the same evidence rule as pricing pieces. Every number,
setting and behaviour comes from a primary page fetched in this run (the vendor's
manual or help centre, the platform's spec, the standard itself). Arithmetic
derived from those numbers is fine and should be shown. Craft advice of my own is
allowed, labelled as my judgement, never presented as a measurement or as
something I tested or heard.

## Voice

Write as Arnau, a working musician who wants the reader to make a good decision.
First person singular for judgement and arithmetic; second person ("you") for
anything the reader does. Calm, direct, a little dry. Never "we", except inside a
vendor quotation.

### What the good music-production publications do, and we copy in spirit

Studied 17 November 2026 on Sound On Sound, Attack Magazine and Bedroom Producers
Blog. Copy the habits, never their sentences:

- **Open on the useful fact, not scene-setting.** Bedroom Producers Blog's first
  sentence names the product and the change. Sound On Sound opens on a pointed
  observation, then the question it raises. Neither warms up.
- **Short paragraphs as the default, one long paragraph where the analysis needs
  room.** Attack runs two to four sentence paragraphs; Sound On Sound lets an
  analytical paragraph run long and then lands it with a single short line.
- **Numbers always in context.** Not "-14 LUFS" alone but what it does to a
  -8 LUFS master. Translate a spec into a consequence.
- **Plain, short headings that signpost.** "Market positioning", "Where loudness
  still wins". Not clever, not questions every time.
- **Steps in prose with signposts** ("start by", "once that's done") unless the
  order is long enough to need numbers.
- **Attribute vendor claims, then judge them.** "According to the company",
  followed by what I make of it. Sceptical of marketing, warm about the tool.
- **Ordinary spoken words.** Contractions, the occasional dry aside, no
  exclamation marks, no marketing adjectives.
- **End on something practical**: a setting to check, a link to the next useful
  piece, a price. Never a summary of what the reader just read.

What we deliberately do NOT copy: their hands-on "we tried it and it sounded
better" narration. They have test rigs and review units; I don't, so quality
judgements are always attributed to whoever made them.

### Every run: calibrate against a live article

Before writing, read one recent article on a related subject from Sound On Sound
(soundonsound.com/techniques), Attack Magazine (attackmagazine.com/technique),
MusicRadar (musicradar.com) or Bedroom Producers Blog (bedroomproducersblog.com).
Note how it opens, how long its paragraphs run and how it handles numbers. Match
that register. Never reuse its sentences, structure or examples, and never cite it
as a source for a fact unless the fact is its own reporting.

## Hard rules

- No em dashes or en dashes, ever.
- Banned: delve, seamless, game-changer, unlock (as marketing), elevate,
  landscape, navigate the world of, when it comes to, it is important to note,
  in conclusion, dive in, robust solution, fast-paced, look no further,
  take your music to the next level. No opening rhetorical question.
- Vary paragraph length. Avoid three-item lists as a reflex, "not X but Y"
  symmetries, closing questions and sentences that restate the paragraph above.
- Never write about failed research. If a fact can't be sourced, it isn't in the
  article.
- Title asks the question or names the subject and never answers it, 60
  characters or fewer. Description 120 to 160 characters (schema max 165), with a
  real number where there is one, not a copy of the first sentence.
