Spotify accepts AI-made music. Beatport says it won't accept tracks that are wholly or substantially AI-generated. The other services in this comparison don't publish a clear AI rule in their distributor agreements.

That leaves a lot of room between 'AI was involved' and 'AI made the whole track'. Here's how the policies draw that line.

## Where each one stands

| Platform | Position on AI-generated music | Where it is written |
|---|---|---|
| Spotify | Accepts it | Public policy statement |
| Beatport | Refuses wholly or substantially AI-generated tracks | Support policy page |
| Bandcamp | No rule about AI tracks. Bans "Unauthorized AI Tools" | Acceptable Use policy |
| DistroKid | No published position | Nothing in the Terms of Service |
| CD Baby | No published position | Nothing in the Artist Agreement |

## Spotify accepts it, and said so in one sentence

The clearest line in these policies is Spotify's: "this is a platform
for licensed music where royalties are paid based on listener engagement, and all music is
treated equally, regardless of the tools used to make it."

So the generator you used is not the issue. Three other things are.

**Impersonation.** "Vocal impersonation is only allowed in music on Spotify when the
impersonated artist has authorized the usage." That is a rule about whose voice it sounds
like, and it applies whether or not you used AI to get there.

**Spam.** Spotify named the tactics it filters for: mass uploads, duplicates, SEO hacks and
artificially short track abuse. It says the system will "identify uploaders and tracks
engaging in these tactics, tag them, and stop recommending them", and that it removed over 75
million spam tracks in twelve months. If you are uploading AI tracks in volume, this is the
rule that will catch you, not an AI rule.

**Disclosure, which is optional.** Spotify supports a DDEX-backed standard for AI credits
submitted through your distributor, and is explicit that it is "not about punishing artists
who use AI responsibly or down-ranking tracks for disclosing". The most useful sentence on the
whole page is the one admitting the limits of that: "Because we depend on artist disclosure,
the absence of a credit doesn't mean AI wasn't used."

## Beatport draws the line at generative, not AI

Beatport is the one clear refusal in this comparison. Its policy "not to accept music on our platform
that has been wholly or substantially generated by AI" is unambiguous, and it extends to using
AI "to clone or replicate the voices or performances of other artists without explicit,
legally cleared permission".

What makes the page genuinely useful is the other half, which most coverage skipped. Beatport
says it is "not discouraging the use of assistive AI tools for mixing, mastering, stem
separation, or standard audio processing". That is a workable distinction, and it is the one
worth carrying around.

If you used [an AI mastering service](/articles/landr-pricing-unlimited-mastering/) or
[a stem separation tool](/articles/ai-stem-separation-costs/), no policy I read treats that
as AI-generated music. Those sit on the left of that line.

## Bandcamp's rule is the one people get wrong

Bandcamp was widely reported as having banned AI music. I read its Acceptable Use and
Moderation Policy, and that is not what it says. There is no clause anywhere in it about
uploading AI-generated tracks, and the phrases "wholly" and "substantially generated" do not
appear.

What it actually prohibits is using the tools. Members agree "Not to create, develop, use, or
offer any Unauthorized AI Tools", and the definition is doing enormous work:

> 'Unauthorized AI Tools' means any artificial intelligence technology or service that uses,
> or is informed by, trained on, or developed using copyrighted works, names, likenesses,
> trademarks, or voices without authorization from the person(s) who own or control such
> copyrights or other rights.

Read that twice. The test is not whether your track sounds generated. It is whether the model
you used was trained on copyrighted material without permission. The policy then extends to
anything "dependent upon an Unauthorized AI Tool".

I'm reporting what the clause says, not predicting how Bandcamp enforces it, and I can't
tell you whether any particular generator meets that definition, because that turns on
training data most vendors do not publish. But the practical point stands: on Bandcamp the
question to ask about your track is what made it, not what it sounds like.

Bandcamp separately bars training any AI model on content from its site, which is a different
rule aimed at scrapers rather than at musicians.

## The distributors say nothing, and that is odd

DistroKid's Terms of Service runs to about 36,000 characters. CD Baby's Artist Agreement runs
to about 64,000. I searched both for AI, artificial intelligence, generative and
machine-generated on 8 November 2026. Zero matches in either. DistroKid's public help centre
returned nothing relevant on the same searches.

The oddity is that both companies appear by name in Spotify's list of partners building the
DDEX AI disclosure standard, alongside Amuse, Believe, RouteNote, EmuBands and others. So both
are publicly involved in standardising how AI use gets declared, while neither has put
anything about AI into the document you actually agree to.

Two things follow, and I won't dress either of them up.

The first is that there is no published distributor rule to report. Anyone telling you
"DistroKid allows AI music" or "DistroKid rejects AI tracks" is describing something that is
not in the terms. Moderation practice can exist without a written rule, and rejections do
happen, but practice is not a policy you can read before you upload.

The second is that silence is not permission. Every one of these agreements already requires
you to hold the rights to what you deliver. That obligation does the work whether or not AI is
mentioned, and it is the clause that bites if a generator's output turns out to infringe.

## What this means before you upload

Check the destination store rather than the distributor. Your distributor is a pipe, and the
rule that stops your track is far more likely to be the shop's. Beatport will refuse a
generated track that Spotify will happily carry.

Do not clone a real singer without written permission. That is the one rule every document
here agrees on, and it is also the one most likely to generate a legal problem rather than
just a takedown.

Volume is a bigger risk than AI. Spotify's spam filter targets behaviour, and uploading
generated tracks in bulk is the behaviour it was built for.

If you are deciding whether you own the output in the first place, that is a separate question
and I looked at one generator's terms in
[Can You Sell Suno Songs?](/articles/can-you-sell-suno-music/).

Everything above is what these documents said on 8 November 2026. Platform AI rules are moving
faster than anything else on this site, and Spotify says outright that it will "continue to
roll out new policies frequently", so re-read the source before you rely on any of it.
