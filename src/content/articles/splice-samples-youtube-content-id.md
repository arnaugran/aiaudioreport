Every Splice subscriber who downloads a loop gets the same licence to it. That is the deal, and it's a good one for making records. It is also the exact property YouTube's Content ID rules are written to exclude, which is why a track built on a popular Splice loop can end up claimed by somebody who never made the loop, and why your own track may not belong in Content ID at all.

Both halves of that come from primary pages: Splice's Terms of Use, last updated on 2 November 2026, Splice's licensing FAQ and certified-licence guide, and YouTube's help centre pages on Content ID eligibility, claims and disputes. I read all of them on 18 November 2026.

## What Splice actually licenses to you

The terms are blunt that "Sounds are licensed, not sold". For music, section 2.3.1.2 grants a "non-exclusive, non-transferable, perpetual right" to use Sounds "in combination with other sounds in music productions" to make what Splice calls New Recordings, for commercial and non-commercial purposes. A second grant, for Creative Works, covers things like games, film and TV including sync, radio, live performance and social video, and there the sounds can be used on their own as effects, loops or sound mixes.

You own what you add. The terms say you own "any original contributions made to the New Recording that are not comprised of Sounds", and then, for the avoidance of doubt, "you will not own the Sounds."

The FAQ spells out the practical consequence. Other users "can also use the same sounds as you without infringing on any New Recording or Creative Works you create using that sound and vice versa." Hold on to that sentence, because it is the whole Content ID problem in miniature.

## What YouTube lets into Content ID

Content ID works from a database of reference files that rights owners submit. Every upload is scanned against it, and a match produces a claim that, depending on the owner's settings, blocks the video, monetises it, or just tracks its viewing figures. YouTube says access is for owners of "exclusive rights to a substantial body of original material".

The eligibility page is where it gets specific. Under a heading called Exclusive rights, it says you must have exclusive rights to the material in the reference file, and lists as ineligible "for use in or as a reference" content "licensed non-exclusively from a third party". Under Distinct reference content, it adds "sound effects, soundbeds, or production loops" to the ineligible list. And matches against so-called royalty-free production music libraries have to be routed to manual review before anyone claims with them. Put the two documents side by side and the rule reads plainly. A Splice sample is non-exclusive by design, and YouTube says non-exclusive material can't be used in a reference.

Splice doesn't argue with this. Its FAQ warns that distributors may have their own monetisation rules, and gives the example that "services may require exclusive rights to all source content (such as individual samples), which is not possible with samples from Splice Sounds."

## So should your track go into Content ID?

My judgement, reading those pages: if a Splice loop is a recognisable element of your track, I wouldn't opt that track into Content ID through a distributor. The risk isn't theoretical. The claim your reference generates lands on every other video that uses the same loop, including ones made by people holding exactly the licence you hold, and YouTube says it can disable references, release their claims, or terminate the partnership of claimants who keep making erroneous ones.

That's a reading of the published rules, not legal advice, and the line between "contains a sample" and "is the sample" isn't drawn on either page. If the Splice material is a hi-hat one-shot buried under a part you played, you're in a different position from someone whose track is a downloaded chord loop with drums on top. The more of the audible track is yours, the stronger your case.

If you release through a distributor that offers Content ID as an add-on, read its own eligibility rules before ticking the box. Splice's FAQ makes the same point about platforms generally, and it's the distributor's form you'll be signing.

## When your video is the one that gets claimed

This is the more common problem, and Splice built a tool for it. From Your Sounds you can select up to 25 samples, choose Generate certified license, enter your legal name and the artist name you release under, and download a PDF that states the licence. Splice's guide says it's free, that you can generate as many as you like, and that it only works for samples you downloaded with credits.

Splice is careful about what the document does. Its FAQ says it can't guarantee your tracks won't get flagged; the certificate is proof that you can use the sample legally, to show a distributor or to support a dispute.

On YouTube, "having all the necessary rights to the content in your video" is one of the valid reasons to dispute a Content ID claim. Giving credit, owning a copy and choosing not to monetise are named as reasons that are not valid. Once you dispute, the claimant is notified and has 30 days to respond. They can release the claim, reinstate it, or let it lapse, and a claim nobody responds to within 30 days expires.

If the claimant reinstates it, you may be able to appeal, and the claimant then has 7 days. So the slowest ordinary path through both stages is 30 plus 7, which is 37 days, before the claimant's remaining option is a formal copyright removal request, which can bring a strike if YouTube finds it valid. YouTube says plainly that it can't decide who's right: both the dispute and the appeal are reviewed by the claimant.

## The five-day window that decides your revenue

This is the detail I'd act on first. YouTube's monetisation page says that if you dispute within 5 days of the claim, the video keeps running ads and all its revenue is held from the day the claim was placed, then paid to whoever wins. Dispute after 5 days and revenue is only held from the day you dispute. Do nothing and, after 5 days, the held revenue is released to the claimant.

In practice that means a video claimed on the 1st and disputed on the 4th keeps every day's earnings held until the dispute is settled. Disputed on the 10th, the nine days before are already gone to the claimant, whatever the outcome. Generating the certificate takes a couple of minutes, so there's no reason to let the window close while you look for paperwork.

Shorts behave differently. YouTube says Shorts longer than one minute with an active copyright claim are blocked, whatever the claim's policy, so a 90-second Short that matches a registered track using your loop simply won't play until the claim is resolved.

## What the licence still forbids

None of this loosens Splice's own rules, and a few of them come up in exactly the situations where people meet Content ID. Section 2.3.1.4 says you may not sublicense Sounds "in isolation as sound effects, loops, or as source material for any other form of sample", even modified; may not redistribute them in new sample packs; may not use them "in a manner competitive" to Splice; and may not use them as training material for AI models. The terms also say NFTs are not Creative Works.

Selling the music is fine. The FAQ says Splice doesn't prevent you distributing New Recordings elsewhere as non-exclusive royalty-free music, leaving it to that platform's rules, but "you may not distribute any Splice Sounds as royalty-free samples or loops." A beat lease is a New Recording. A folder of your chopped Splice loops sold as a kit is not.

There's also a rule about people. You can't use the name, image or likeness of the artist behind a Sound without their written permission, and the terms name crediting them as a featured artist as one of the prohibited uses. The FAQ adds that you don't need to credit Splice or the sample's creator at all, including when you register with a PRO, though most PROs want you to disclose that third-party samples are in the song.

One housekeeping note: the FAQ still points readers to "Section 3.1.1.3" for the prohibited uses, while the terms as updated in November 2026 number that list 2.3.1.4. The terms are the contract, so cite their numbering if you ever need to quote it to a distributor.

## The habit worth building

Generate the certified licence when you finish the track, not when the claim arrives. Name the PDF after the release, keep it with the stems, and you'll have it inside the 5 days that matter.
