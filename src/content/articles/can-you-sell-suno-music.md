If you pay for Suno's Pro or Premier tier and you downloaded the track through Suno
itself, the contract says you can sell it. If you are on the free tier, the same contract
says you cannot. That is the short answer, and it is the easy part.

The hard part is that "can I sell this?" is three separate questions wearing a trench
coat, and nearly every page answering it picks one and implies it settled the other two.

## Three questions, not one

1. **Does Suno's contract permit commercial use?** This is a question about an agreement
   between you and a company. Suno controls the answer and can change it, and in August
   2026 it did.
2. **Does a copyright exist in the track at all?** This is a question about law, not
   contract. Suno has no say in it, and says so itself.
3. **Will the platform you upload to accept it and keep it up?** A question about
   distributor and streaming-service policy, which is stricter than either of the above
   and moves faster.

You can comfortably get a yes on the first and a no on the second. That combination is
not a contradiction, and understanding why is most of what you need.

## Question one: what Suno's contract says

Suno's terms were last revised on 10 August 2026 and took effect on 3 September 2026,
which is after most of the advice currently ranking for this question was written. The
tier split is explicit.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Your tier | What the terms grant |
| --- | --- |
| Free / Basic | You "will only use such Outputs for your lawful, personal and non-commercial purposes." |
| Pro / Premier | "Suno hereby assigns to you all of its right, title and interest in and to any Output owned by Suno", subject to the terms and their commercial-use restrictions. |

</div>

Read the Pro and Premier wording closely, because the drafting is careful in a way that
matters. Suno assigns the rights **it owns**. In the same section it adds that "Suno
makes no representation or warranty to you that any copyright will vest in any Output."

That is a company saying, in its own contract: we are handing you whatever we have, and
we are not promising that what we have is anything at all. It is not sharp practice —
given where the law currently sits it is the only honest thing Suno could write. But it
is the single most important sentence in the document, and it is missing from almost
every summary of it.

### Commercial rights attach to downloads, not songs

This is the detail that catches people, and it is worth more attention than the tier
table.

Under the Permitted Commercial Use section, you may commercially exploit Output "solely
to the extent it adheres to Suno's Conditions of Access and Use", and only where you
have a permitted Download. The terms then close the obvious loophole: "You may not
commercially exploit Output that has not been downloaded by you through an approved
channel", and "Obtaining a copy of an Output by any means other than a download channel
made available by Suno is prohibited."

So the unit of commercial rights is the download, not the song, and the number of
downloads you get each month depends on your tier. Pulling the audio out some other way —
capturing the stream, grabbing the file from a network request, re-recording the playback
— produces a file you are not permitted to commercially exploit, even while paying for a
tier that would otherwise allow it.

The flip side is genuinely good news, and nobody seems to mention it. Commercial use
rights for a Download are **perpetual**, and the terms say they are unaffected by "the
expiry, cancellation, downgrade or suspension of your subscription." Pay for one month,
download your tracks properly, cancel, and the tracks you downloaded stay commercially
usable. You are not renting the rights to your own back catalogue.

### Two restrictions that are easy to walk into

**Remixes are a different animal.** Under the terms a Remix is "a joint work owned
jointly and equally by you and the Remixer", and — this is the part that bites — it "may
only be used for lawful, personal and non-commercial purposes, regardless of your
subscription tier." Regardless of tier. Your Premier subscription does not make a remix
commercially usable, and a joint work means a co-owner you may not have thought about.

**You cannot use Output to compete with Suno.** The User Conduct section says "In no
event will you use the Output or your Voice Model to compete with Suno." If your plan
involves building anything adjacent to a music-generation product, that clause is a
problem long before copyright is.

There is also a broad restriction on commercially exploiting "any portion of the
Service" itself — reselling access, transferring it, granting others access. Selling
tracks is permitted; reselling Suno is not.

## Question two: is there a copyright to own?

Here the contract stops mattering and US law takes over.

In January 2025 the US Copyright Office published Part 2 of its report on copyright and
AI, dealing specifically with copyrightability. Its conclusions are stated plainly on
page iii, and two of them decide this question:

> Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is
> insufficient human control over the expressive elements.

> Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not
> alone provide sufficient control.

If your process was typing a prompt and keeping the result you liked, the Office's
position is that there is no copyright in that output. Not a weak copyright. None. Suno
can assign you every right it holds and the total can still be zero, which is exactly
what its no-warranty sentence is quietly telling you.

What this does **not** mean is that AI-assisted music is unprotectable. The same page
says:

> The use of AI tools to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect
> the availability of copyright protection for the output.

> Human authors are entitled to copyright in their works of authorship that are
> perceptible in AI-generated outputs, as well as the creative selection, coordination,
> or arrangement of material in the outputs, or creative modifications of the outputs.

So the lever you actually control is how much human authorship is in the finished record.
Lyrics you wrote are yours and remain yours when a model sings them. A generated stem
chopped, re-arranged, re-performed and mixed into a larger piece makes that selection and
arrangement yours. Generated material edited substantially enough to count as creative
modification brings those modifications inside the copyright.

The Office is explicit that this is "analyzed on a case-by-case basis", and that existing
law handles it "without the need for legislative change" — meaning nobody should be
waiting for a new statute to clarify things.

One scope warning. Everything in this section is the position of the **US** Copyright
Office. We have not checked how the UK, the EU or any other jurisdiction treats the same
question, and we are not going to imply we have. If you are releasing outside the US,
that is a separate piece of homework.

### Why the distinction has teeth

Contract rights let you sell. Copyright lets you stop somebody else from selling the same
thing. If a track has no copyright, you can put it up and take the money, and you have no
mechanism against a stranger who downloads it and uploads it as their own. For background
music on a video, nobody cares. For a release you intend to build on, sync, or defend,
the gap is the whole ball game.

## Question three: will the platform keep it up?

Both previous answers are irrelevant if the track gets pulled.

Spotify's position, announced on 25 September 2025, is not anti-AI, but it is specific.
Three things from that announcement matter if you are planning a release:

- **Impersonation.** "Vocal impersonation is only allowed in music on Spotify when the
  impersonated artist has authorized the usage." A convincing imitation of a known singer
  is the fastest route to a takedown, whatever your subscription permits.
- **Spam.** Spotify said it was rolling out a music spam filter targeting mass uploads,
  duplicates and similar tactics, and stated it had "removed over 75 million spammy
  tracks from Spotify" in the previous twelve months. Volume is itself a risk factor. One
  considered release behaves very differently from forty generated tracks in a week.
- **Disclosure.** Spotify is backing a DDEX-based industry standard for AI credits, so
  disclosures made through your label or distributor can appear in song credits. Spotify
  framed this as not down-ranking tracks for disclosing. Disclosure is the low-risk
  choice.

Distributors sit in front of Spotify and apply their own screening, which is where most
rejections actually happen. We have not been able to verify any individual distributor's
current AI policy from a primary source, and the third-party write-ups we found disagree
with each other about how strict each one is — so we are not going to repeat their
numbers. Check your distributor's own help pages before you schedule a release date
around the answer.

## What we would actually do

If you are putting out a record with generated material in it:

Pay for the tier that permits commercial use, and download through Suno's own download
button. Not the stream, not a workaround. Keep the files — the rights attached to those
downloads survive your cancelling the subscription.

Put real human authorship into the record, because that is the only part of the copyright
question you control. Your lyrics, your arrangement choices, your edits, your mix. This
is not a trick to game the Copyright Office; it is the actual distinction the Office
drew, and it also tends to produce better records.

Do not remix other people's Suno tracks and plan to sell the result. The terms block it
regardless of what you pay.

Do not imitate a recognisable singer. Disclose AI use through your distributor. Release
at a human pace.

And assume all of this has an expiry date. Suno's terms changed in August 2026 and will
change again. We will re-read them and date-stamp this page when they do — that is the
whole reason the date is at the top.
