If you pay for Suno Pro or Premier, and you downloaded the track through Suno itself, the
contract says you can sell it. On the free tier it says you can't. That part is simple.

The hard part is that "can I sell this?" is really three questions. Most pages answering it
pick one, answer that, and leave you assuming the other two are settled as well.

## Three questions, not one

1. **Does Suno's contract let you use it commercially?** That's an agreement between you and
   a company. Suno decides, and Suno can change it. In August 2026 it did.
2. **Is there a copyright in the track at all?** That's a question of law. Suno has no say in
   it, and says so itself.
3. **Will the platform you upload to accept it and keep it there?** Distributor and streaming
   policy, which is stricter than either of the above and changes faster.

You can get a yes on the first and a no on the second. That isn't a contradiction, and
understanding why is most of what you need.

## Question one: what Suno's contract says

Suno last revised its terms on 10 August 2026, effective 3 September 2026. That's after most
of the advice currently ranking for this question was written. The split between tiers is
explicit.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Your tier | What the terms grant |
| --- | --- |
| Free / Basic | You "will only use such Outputs for your lawful, personal and non-commercial purposes." |
| Pro / Premier | "Suno hereby assigns to you all of its right, title and interest in and to any Output owned by Suno", subject to the terms and their commercial use restrictions. |

</div>

Read the Pro and Premier wording closely. Suno assigns the rights **it owns**. A few lines
later it adds that "Suno makes no representation or warranty to you that any copyright will
vest in any Output."

That is a company saying, in its own contract: we are giving you whatever we have, and we are
not promising that what we have is anything at all. It isn't sharp practice. Given where the
law currently sits, it's close to the only honest thing they could write. But it's the most
important sentence in the document, and it's missing from nearly every summary of it.

### Commercial rights attach to downloads, not songs

This is the part that catches people out, and it deserves more attention than the tier table.

Under Permitted Commercial Use you can commercially exploit Output "solely to the extent it
adheres to Suno's Conditions of Access and Use", and only where you have a permitted
Download. The terms then close the obvious loophole: "You may not commercially exploit Output
that has not been downloaded by you through an approved channel", and "Obtaining a copy of an
Output by any means other than a download channel made available by Suno is prohibited."

So the unit of commercial rights is the download, not the song, and how many downloads you
get each month depends on your tier. Pull the audio out some other way, by capturing the
stream or grabbing the file from a network request, and you end up with a file you aren't
permitted to sell, even while paying for a tier that would otherwise allow it.

The good news rarely gets mentioned. Commercial rights on a Download are perpetual, and the
terms say they survive "the expiry, cancellation, downgrade or suspension of your
subscription." Pay for one month, download your tracks properly, cancel, and those tracks
stay commercially usable. You aren't renting the rights to your own back catalogue.

### Two restrictions that are easy to walk into

**Remixes work differently.** A Remix is "a joint work owned jointly and equally by you and
the Remixer", and it "may only be used for lawful, personal and non-commercial purposes,
regardless of your subscription tier." Regardless of tier. Premier doesn't make a remix
sellable, and a joint work means a co-owner you may not have thought about.

**You can't use Output to compete with Suno.** The User Conduct section says "In no event
will you use the Output or your Voice Model to compete with Suno." If your plan involves
building anything close to a music generation product, that clause matters long before
copyright does.

There's also a broad restriction on commercially exploiting "any portion of the Service"
itself: reselling access, transferring it, granting others access. Selling tracks is fine.
Reselling Suno isn't.

## Question two: is there a copyright to own?

Here the contract stops mattering and US law takes over.

In January 2025 the US Copyright Office published Part 2 of its report on copyright and AI,
covering copyrightability. Its conclusions sit on page iii, and two of them settle this
question:

> Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is
> insufficient human control over the expressive elements.

> Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone
> provide sufficient control.

If your process was typing a prompt and keeping the result you liked, the Office's position
is that there's no copyright in that output. Not a weak copyright. None. Suno can assign you
every right it holds and the total can still be zero, which is what that no-warranty sentence
is quietly telling you.

That doesn't mean AI-assisted music can't be protected. The same page says:

> The use of AI tools to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect the
> availability of copyright protection for the output.

> Human authors are entitled to copyright in their works of authorship that are perceptible
> in AI-generated outputs, as well as the creative selection, coordination, or arrangement of
> material in the outputs, or creative modifications of the outputs.

So the lever you control is how much human authorship ends up in the finished record. Lyrics
you wrote stay yours when a model sings them. A generated stem that you chop, rearrange,
re-perform and mix into something larger makes that selection and arrangement yours.
Generated material you edit substantially enough to count as creative modification brings
those edits inside the copyright.

The Office is clear that this gets decided "on a case-by-case basis", and that existing law
can handle it "without the need for legislative change". Nobody should be waiting for a new
statute to sort this out.

One limit on all of that: it's the position of the **US** Copyright Office. We haven't
checked how the UK, the EU or anywhere else treats the same question, and we aren't going to
imply we have. Releasing outside the US is separate homework.

### Why the distinction has teeth

Contract rights let you sell. Copyright lets you stop someone else selling the same thing. If
a track has no copyright, you can put it up and take the money, and you have nothing to use
against a stranger who downloads it and releases it as their own. For background music on a
video, that probably doesn't matter. For a release you want to build on, license for sync, or
defend, it's the whole game.

## Question three: will the platform keep it up?

Both answers above are academic if the track gets pulled.

Spotify set out its position on 25 September 2025. It isn't anti-AI, but it is specific.
Three things from that announcement matter if you're planning a release.

**Impersonation.** "Vocal impersonation is only allowed in music on Spotify when the
impersonated artist has authorized the usage." A convincing imitation of a known singer is
the fastest route to a takedown, whatever your subscription permits.

**Spam.** Spotify said it was rolling out a music spam filter aimed at mass uploads,
duplicates and similar tactics, and that it had "removed over 75 million spammy tracks from
Spotify" in the previous twelve months. Volume on its own is a risk factor. One considered
release looks nothing like forty generated tracks in a week.

**Disclosure.** Spotify is backing a DDEX-based industry standard for AI credits, so
disclosures made through your label or distributor can appear in song credits. Spotify said
it isn't down-ranking tracks for disclosing. Disclosing is the low-risk option.

Distributors sit in front of Spotify and run their own screening, and that's where most
rejections actually happen. We couldn't verify any individual distributor's current AI policy
from a primary source, and the third-party write-ups we found disagree with each other about
how strict each one is, so we're not going to repeat their numbers. Check your distributor's
own help pages before you build a release date around the answer.

## What we'd actually do

If you're putting out a record with generated material in it:

Pay for a tier that permits commercial use, and download through Suno's own download button.
Not the stream, not a workaround. Keep the files, because the rights attached to those
downloads survive you cancelling the subscription.

Put real human work into the record, because that's the only part of the copyright question
you control. Your lyrics, your arrangement choices, your edits, your mix. This isn't a trick
to game the Copyright Office. It's the actual line the Office drew, and it tends to produce
better records anyway.

Don't remix someone else's Suno track and plan to sell the result. The terms block it
whatever you pay.

Don't imitate a recognisable singer. Disclose AI use through your distributor. Release at a
human pace.

And assume all of this has an expiry date. Suno's terms changed in August 2026 and will
change again. We'll re-read them and update the date at the top when they do. That's what the
date is for.
