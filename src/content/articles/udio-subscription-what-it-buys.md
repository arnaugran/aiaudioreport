Udio still sells subscriptions at $10 and $30 a month, and its plan comparison still lists dozens of creative tools. What it no longer sells is a file. Downloading of audio, video and stems was switched off when the company announced its partnership with Universal Music Group on 29 October 2025, and the Terms of Service that followed, last revised on 12 November 2025, turn that button change into a contract.

So the useful question about the price has changed. It used to be how many songs $10 buys. Now it's what you can do with them once they exist, and the answer shapes whether a producer on a budget should pay at all.

I read Udio's terms, its pricing page and six of its help centre articles on 21 November 2026. Every figure below comes from those pages.

## What the terms let you do with a song

Section 1.2 is the one that matters. You "may not download copies of any Output", and you agree not to "download, reproduce, transmit, display, publish or otherwise distribute" it on a personal device or on any streaming or user-generated platform. Udio names the platforms itself: YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Spotify and SoundCloud.

The one exception is sharing hyperlinks to songs on approved third-party sites, and Udio says that approval can be revoked at any time.

Section 6.3 sets the ceiling on everything else. You "may use Output solely for your personal and non-commercial purposes", again with the rider that you may not download it "for any purpose". The list of prohibited uses in section 5.2 adds exploiting the Services or any Output "for any commercial purpose".

None of that changes with a paid plan. The only place the terms treat subscribers differently on the use of Output is section 6.4, and it runs the other way from what you might hope. Anyone who makes public use of Output has to credit Udio prominently, in the track title or the credits, unless the Output was generated during a paid subscription. A subscription buys you out of the credit line. It doesn't buy you the right to release.

Ownership is covered in my [piece on selling Suno music](/articles/can-you-sell-suno-music/), where Udio's assignment clause sits beside Suno's opposite approach. The short version is that section 6.1 says Udio and its licensors own the Output, and that anything that might vest in you is assigned to them.

## What the money buys

The plan comparison on Udio's pricing page, read on 21 November 2026, in US dollars:

| Plan | Monthly price | Billed annually | Monthly credits | Songs at once |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Free | $0 | $0 | 100, plus 10 a day | 4 |
| Standard | $10 | $96 ($8 a month) | 2,400 | 6 |
| Pro | $30 | $288 ($24 a month) | 6,000 | 10 |

Credits are limits, not a balance. They reset each month and don't roll over. Credits bought separately do roll over and never expire, at $3 for 100 or $25 for 1,000.

Udio's credits article explains the unit. Every click of Create, Extend, Remix, Inpaint or Edit makes two songs. A pair of 32-second songs costs two credits; a pair of 130-second songs costs four. Trimming is free.

Here's my arithmetic on that. Standard's 2,400 credits make 600 two-minute generations, or 1,200 songs, a month. On the monthly price that's $10 divided by 600, about 1.7 cents a generation; on annual billing it's $8 divided by 600, about 1.3 cents. Spread across a 30-day month, 600 generations is 20 a day, which is more than most people will sit and audition.

Pro is where the numbers get odd. Six thousand credits for $30 works out at 0.5 cents a credit, against roughly 0.42 cents on monthly Standard. The bigger plan costs more per credit, not less.

What the extra $20 actually adds, according to the comparison table, is 3,600 more credits, ten songs generating at once instead of six, and one feature Standard lacks: adjusting song access permissions. Udio's help centre describes that as choosing, song by song, whether other users may remix, extend or style your tracks. Every other line in the table, from Voices to Blend Styles to uploading your own audio, carries a tick for both paid plans. Set that against the top-up packs and Pro starts to make a case for itself. At $25 for 1,000, a bought credit costs 2.5 cents, six times what a Standard credit costs, while Pro's extra 3,600 credits for $20 come to about 0.56 cents each. If you run out of Standard credits every month, the bigger plan is the cheaper fix; if it happens once a year, a $3 pack is.

One housekeeping note. The answer about expiring credits in the FAQ on Udio's pricing page still gives "1200 credits for standard" as its example, while the table above it says 2,400. The UMG help article explains the gap: Standard went from 1,200 to 2,400 and Pro from 4,800 to 6,000 as part of the deal. The table is current.

## The trial that bills a year

This is the detail I'd check before clicking anything. Udio offers one 7-day trial of Standard per person. Its help page says that if you don't cancel before the seven days are up, you are "immediately charged the full price of an annual subscription" unless you had toggled to monthly beforehand. Udio's own example puts that at $96 plus tax in the US.

The trial also isn't quite Standard. It gives you the Standard features but keeps the free credit limits, 10 a day, and the free cap of three 130-second songs a day. You can't judge the 2,400-credit plan from inside it.

If you do subscribe, the terms say payments are non-refundable and there's no credit for a part-used period. Section 3.3 says that to avoid the next charge you must cancel 7 days before the renewal date, through account settings or by email. The help centre says a cancellation takes effect at the end of the current cycle and you keep full access until then. Upgrades are prorated and immediate; downgrades wait for the next cycle.

## Before you upload your own audio

Uploading your own audio to generate from is a paid-plan feature, so read section 6.3 before you use it.

Anything you submit counts as Your Content, and you grant Udio a licence to it that the terms call "royalty free, fully paid-up, transferable, sub-licensable, assignable, worldwide, perpetual and irrevocable". It covers training Udio's models and use "in connection with the use, monetization, advertising, promotion, marketing, and improvement" of its products, and the terms say no compensation is due to you.

Section 1.1 repeats the training point: Udio may use your inputs and the outputs to train its AI. My judgement is simple. Don't upload a stem, a vocal or an unreleased idea you'd mind seeing in a training set, because the licence can't be withdrawn once granted.

## Who should still pay

For a producer whose goal is releasable material, I don't think either paid plan is worth it today. You'd be paying for songs you can't download, can't upload to a distributor, and can't use commercially, on a plan whose one release-related perk is skipping a credit you aren't allowed to need.

Someone who makes songs for the pleasure of it and shares them as udio.com links has a different calculation, and for them Standard on annual billing is the sensible tier. The per-credit price is lower than Pro's, and 20 two-minute generations a day is plenty for listening and sharing.

The other case is a bet on what comes next. Udio's Warner help article says its "reimagined platform" will let users make remixes, covers and new songs with the voices and styles of artists who choose to take part. It also says that for now the service is governed by the transition terms put in place on 29 October. Section 10.2 lets Udio change those terms at any time, with the new version effective from posting.

Paying in advance for rights that haven't been written yet isn't something I'd do on a budget.

If you want AI-generated parts you can actually release, the [Suno terms](/articles/can-you-sell-suno-music/) are the ones to read next, followed by [distributors' rules on AI music](/articles/can-you-upload-ai-music/). And if you start the Udio trial anyway, switch the toggle on the pricing page to Monthly before you begin, so a forgotten cancellation costs $10 rather than $96.
