Native Instruments asks nothing more of you when a track built on Komplete or Kontakt sounds goes out on Spotify, into a sync library or onto a beat store. No fee per release, no credit, no form. The restrictions all sit on the other side of the line, around what happens to the sounds themselves, and around a few product labels most people never look at.

I read the four documents that settle this on 25 November 2026: NI's End User License Agreement, which carries the version date 2026-07-01 and the name of the current owner, inMusic NI GmbH in Berlin; the separate subscription terms for NI 360, effective the same day; the NI 360 FAQ; and the short support article NI keeps for exactly this question. They agree with each other closely, which is not something I can say for every company's paperwork.

## The permission

Section 3.7 of the EULA attaches a Sound License Agreement to any product that includes a sound library. Its first sentence is the one you need: the samples, instruments and presets "can be used for commercial or non-commercial music and audio productions without the prior permission from Native Instruments."

The support article says it more bluntly. All samples, instruments and presets provided by NI "may be used for commercial music production and compositions anytime without any further permission."

So a song, an album, a beat you lease, a score for a client's film or a podcast theme are all covered. Nothing in either text asks for attribution.

## What you can't do with the sounds

The same paragraph of the EULA closes the obvious loophole. Using the product "for the creation of a sound library or as a sound library for any kind of synthesizer, virtual instrument, sample library, sample-based product or other musical instrument is strictly prohibited." Individual samples, sound sets and loops may not be distributed standalone, "commercially or otherwise", and may not be "repackaged in whole or in part as audio samples, sound libraries or sound effects."

NI's support article adds the detail producers tend to argue about. The prohibition covers "single, combined or modified sounds". Running a Massive X patch through three effects and selling the renders as one-shots is still selling NI's sounds, and so is a preset bank for another synth built from resampled Kontakt notes.

"Or otherwise" means free is no escape. The subscription terms spell this out with a longer verb list: you must not "sell, rent, loan, lend, give away, sublicense, transfer or otherwise distribute" the content as a standalone product, "but only as incorporated in your music and audio production." A free loop pack offered to grow a mailing list is a giveaway of standalone content.

The beat-selling question sorts itself out the same way. My reading is that a finished instrumental leased to an artist is your music and audio production, so it sits inside the permission. A "melody loop kit" sold to other producers so they can build their own tracks from it is a sample-based product, and that is the prohibited list almost word for word.

## Three labels that switch the permission off

Section 3.8 of the EULA carves out three kinds of product, and the commercial permission above doesn't help you with any of them.

EDU versions "shall only be used by students or those working in educational institutions" and "must not be used for commercial purposes". If you bought Komplete at the student price and now take paid mixing work or release music for money, the licence you hold doesn't cover that. The NI 360 subscription terms carry the same rule for EDU versions of the subscription.

NFR (Not For Resale) products and anything labelled a Demo Version are limited to "demonstration, testing and evaluation purposes". The Demo clause says outright that they must not be used commercially.

The Demo label matters more than it seems, because of how the free Kontakt Player works. NI's product page says instruments not made for Kontakt 8 Player "can be used for 15 minutes at a time in demo mode and without the ability to save your work", and the Player manual says features running in demo mode are marked with a DEMO badge on the interface. NI's texts don't connect that badge to the EULA's Demo Version clause in so many words. My judgement is that a sound that only plays because of a 15-minute demo timer is the clearest example of evaluation use there is, and I wouldn't print it into a release. If a part matters, buy the full Kontakt or pick an instrument that loads in the Player without the badge.

The free Komplete Start bundle, which NI's Kontakt Player page lists with instruments such as Analog Dreams and Irish Harp, is a different case. The EULA's restricted labels are EDU, NFR and Demo Version, and the Sound License Agreement attaches to any product that includes a sound library. My reading is that a free bundle carrying none of those labels gets the same commercial permission as a paid one.

## Third-party libraries

Some content inside NI products belongs to other companies. NI's support article says that in this case the third party's licence and copyright terms "are legally binding", that those licences "do vary", and that you may also use this content for commercial music production without further permission.

That statement covers third-party content shipped inside NI products. A library you bought straight from another developer, even one that runs in Kontakt Player, comes with that developer's own licence. Open it before you assume NI's wording applies.

## NI 360: licensed for as long as you pay

The subscription changes one thing that matters. The licence grant in the subscription terms is "for the duration of your subscription", and the terms add a condition the EULA doesn't have: your production "must qualify as a creative work", with compositions, tracks, melodies, songs, albums and background scores given as examples.

Cancellation takes effect at the end of the current term, and the terms say access to and use of the services will be restricted from then. The 360 FAQ is more specific about what that does to a session. The instruments and effects stop working. Settings and presets are kept in their last saved state, audio continues to pass through effects, and "most instruments will work for 15 minutes, enabling you to bounce individual tracks or your project." NI adds that it is working on extending this behaviour to all instruments and effects, which tells you some don't do it yet.

Neither document says anything about music you already released. The grant is time-limited and the termination language is about access to the software and content, so my reading is that a song you finished and distributed while subscribed doesn't have to be withdrawn when you stop paying. What you lose is the ability to keep working on projects that still depend on live 360 instruments.

There is a second way to lose access without choosing to. The FAQ says you must go online and open Native Access once a month so the subscription can renew itself. A laptop that lives offline for a long tour can find its instruments stopped.

That makes the 15-minute window the practical point of the whole subscription question. It's generous enough for a single song, and it's a poor plan for a back catalogue of open sessions, especially as it only applies to "most instruments". I'd print every 360 part to audio as each song is finished, rather than leaving it to the day you cancel.

## Devices, resale and AI training

A few other EULA clauses affect a working producer. You may install a perpetual licence on three devices, "provided that the said software is used only on one device regularly", and simultaneous use on more than one machine is not permitted. The 360 terms give the same three devices, used one at a time. Two producers sharing one account in two studios is outside both.

Renting or lending the software is "expressly forbidden". Reselling a perpetual licence is allowed: the buyer has to agree to the EULA in writing, you remove every copy and de-register the product, NI transfers the serial number on request from your registered email, and it reserves the right to charge a fee. Bundles can only be resold as a whole, NFR and EDU products not at all.

Both the EULA and the subscription terms also forbid using the product's audio content for training artificial intelligence. The EULA names data mining under Section 44b of the German Copyright Act as well. If you build audio tools, NI's libraries are not a dataset.

## Before you release

Check what kind of licence each NI product you used actually is. Native Access shows your products, and an EDU or NFR serial is the one thing that turns a finished track into a problem. If any part was played by an instrument showing the DEMO badge, replace it or buy the licence before the track goes out.

If you're on NI 360, bounce stems of every song in progress now, not in the last week of your term. And if you're comparing NI's rules with other libraries, my piece on [GarageBand and Logic loops](/articles/garageband-logic-loops-commercial-use/) covers Apple's near-identical repackaging clause, and the one on [Splice samples and YouTube Content ID](/articles/splice-samples-youtube-content-id/) covers what happens when everyone owns the same sound.
